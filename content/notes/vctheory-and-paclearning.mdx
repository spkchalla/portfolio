## Learning from Samples

The goal of learning from samples is to learn an unknown function $f$ from as few samples as possible. We receive inputs $x$ drawn i.i.d. from a distribution and query the oracle (a black box). The oracle outputs the value $f(x)$. From these input-output pairs, we want to reconstruct or approximately learn the function.

Let us take a specific example and learn a toy function under specific conditions.

Suppose $\mathcal{X}$ is a finite domain, the inputs are drawn uniformly from $\mathcal{X}$, and $f : \mathcal{X} \to \{-1,+1\}$. We are promised that $f$ is either constant on the entire domain or assigns each label to exactly half of the domain. That is, either

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{A.}\quad &\exists c \in \{-1,+1\} \quad \text{such that} \quad
                  f(x)=c \quad \forall x \in \mathcal{X},
\\[4pt]
&\text{or}
\\[4pt]
\text{B.}\quad &\left|f^{-1}(-1)\right|
                  = \left|f^{-1}(+1)\right|
                  = \frac{|\mathcal{X}|}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, we have two options, and I need to figure out which kind of function the oracle represents. I draw samples and look at their outputs. The outputs may be different, or they may all be the same. If I observe both $+1$ and $-1$, I can immediately say that the function is balanced because of the promise. If all the outputs are the same, however, I cannot be certain. My learning rule will declare the function constant in that case, so all I can talk about is the probability that this decision is wrong.

For example, let us say that I have drawn three samples and all are in the $+1$ class. Can I say that the function is constant? No. A balanced function could also produce those three outputs. Nor can I say for sure that it is balanced and that drawing more samples will reveal this. My whole issue is to figure out how many samples I need before making a decision, so I must choose that number in advance.

Then comes the idea of probability. If the function is balanced, each draw has probability $1/2$ of producing either label. Therefore, the probability that three draws are all $+1$ is

$$
\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3 = \frac{1}{8}.
$$

The same is true for three outputs that are all $-1$. Thus, the probability that all three outputs are the same, causing my learning rule to mistake a balanced function for a constant one, is

$$
2\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3 = \frac{2}{8} = \frac{1}{4}.
$$

So, now comes the big question: how much risk of being fooled by the sampled observations can I afford? I shall call this limit $\delta$, where $0 < \delta < 1$.

If I draw $n$ samples, the probability that a balanced function produces only one label is

$$
2\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n = 2^{1-n}.
$$

Therefore, I will choose enough samples that

$$
2^{1-n} \leq \delta.
$$

This makes sure that the probability of my learning rule being fooled by the sampled observations is at most $\delta$.

Equivalently, I need

$$
n \geq 1 + \log_2\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right).
$$

Since the number of samples must be an integer, the smallest such choice is $n = \left\lceil 1 + \log_2(1/\delta) \right\rceil$.

We can distinguish between case A and case B with probability atleast $1-\delta$ using $O(log\frac{1}{8})$ queries. $\delta$ $\ge 0$ under finite no. of samples.

## Goals in Learning Theory

Given a function $f : \mathbb{X} \to \mathbb{R}$, and knowing that $f \in \mathcal{F}$, where $\mathcal{F}$ is a set of structured functions, we want to **learn** $f$ using as few queries as possible to the black box, with a success probability of at least $(1-\delta)$.

Ideally, we want to learn $f$ exactly, but that is quite hard to achieve.

Here, **learning** means that, with probability at least $1-\delta$ over the sampled observations, your algorithm will output some $\tilde{f} \in \mathcal{F}$ such that
$$
\mathbb{P}_{x}(\tilde{f}(x) \neq f(x)) \leq \epsilon.
$$

This is called $(\epsilon, \delta)$-PAC learning. We need to figure out $\tilde{f}$. For this, we need to know the sample complexity. Sample complexity is denoted by
$$
\mathcal{s}(\epsilon, \delta)
$$
which is the number of i.i.d. samples from $\mathbb{X}$ I need to learn $f$ with these guarantees. If the sample complexity is higher, then the function class is harder to learn.

## Inner product, Affine functions and Hyperplanes

### Inner Product
Let us say $\mathbb{X}\subseteq \mathbb{R}^{N}$. Given two points $\bar x, \bar y \in \mathbb{R}^{N}$. 
$\langle \bar x, \bar y \rangle$ denotes the inner product between $\bar {x} \quad \& \quad \bar {y}$.
Inner product is defined as 
$$
\langle \bar x, \bar y \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{N}(\bar x_{i} \bar y_{i})
$$
where $\bar x = (\bar x_1, \bar x_2, \dots, \bar x_N)$ and $\bar y = (\bar y_1, \bar y_2, \dots, \bar y_N)$
### Affine functions over $\mathbb{R}^{N}$
An affine function $\mathcal{f_{\bar a, b}}(\bar x)$ is defined as 
$$
\mathcal{f_{\bar a, b}}(\bar x) = \langle \bar a, \bar x \rangle + b
$$
where $\bar a \in \mathbb{R^{N}} \quad \& \quad b \in \mathbb{R}$
### Hyperplanes

An affine function assigns a real value to every point in $\mathbb{R}^N$. A natural question to ask is: **what is the set of points that receive the same value under this function?** In particular, the set of points where the affine function evaluates to zero forms a geometric object known as a **hyperplane**.

Given an affine function
$$
\mathcal{f}_{\bar a,b}(\bar x)=\langle \bar a,\bar x\rangle+b,
$$
the **hyperplane** induced by this affine function is the set of all points whose function value is zero. Formally,
$$
H=\left\{\bar x\in\mathbb{R}^N:\langle \bar a,\bar x\rangle+b=0\right\}.
$$

The hyperplane partitions the space into two half-spaces:
$$
\langle \bar a,\bar x\rangle+b>0
$$
and
$$
\langle \bar a,\bar x\rangle+b<0.
$$

Thus, every point in $\mathbb{R}^N$ either lies on the hyperplane or belongs to one of the two half-spaces.
## Radon's Theorem

The theorem is referred from convex geometry.

Let $P_{1},P_{2},P_{3} \dots P_{N},P_{N+1},P_{N+2}$ be points in $\mathbb{R}^{N}$. Then $\exists$ two partitions $S_{1}$ and $S_{2}$ of the aove points such that $S_{1}$ and $S_{2}$ of the above points **cannot be separated by a linear classifier**.
This means that we can make such partitions for which we can never make a linear classifier to partition them. (think of yin-yang diagram).

$$
S_{1} \cap  S_{2} = \phi \\
S_{1} \neq \phi \\
S_{2} \neq \phi \\

S_{1} \cup S_{2} = {P_{1},P_{2},P_{3} \dots P_{N},P_{N+1},P_{N+2}}
$$

### Proof

Using the fact that any $N+1$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^{N}$ are linearly **DEPENDENT**, we can show that $\quad \exists \quad \lambda_{1}, \lambda_{2}, \dots, \lambda_{N+2}$ in $\mathbb{R}$ not all $\lambda$ being zeros, such that
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{N+2}(\lambda_{i} P_i) = 0\\
\text{and}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{N+2} (\lambda_i) = 0
$$

Let us say $P_1$ is origin and $v_i = P_{i+1} - P_1 , \forall i \in [N+1]$
Since
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{N+2}\lambda_i=0,
$$
there must exist both positive and negative coefficients (otherwise all the coefficients would have to be zero).

Define
$$
L^-=\{\,i\mid \lambda_i<0\,\},
\qquad
L^+=\{\,i\mid \lambda_i>0\,\}.
$$

Now partition the given points into
$$
S_1=\{P_i\mid i\in L^-\},
\qquad
S_2=\{P_j\mid j\in L^+\}.
$$

Using
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{N+2}\lambda_iP_i=0,
$$
we obtain
$$
\sum_{i\in L^+}\lambda_iP_i
=
\sum_{j\in L^-}(-\lambda_j)P_j.
$$

Let
$$
\alpha
=
\sum_{i\in L^+}\lambda_i
=
\sum_{j\in L^-}(-\lambda_j),
$$
where the equality follows from
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{N+2}\lambda_i=0.
$$

Dividing both sides by $\alpha$, we get
$$
\sum_{i\in L^+}\frac{\lambda_i}{\alpha}P_i
=
\sum_{j\in L^-}\frac{-\lambda_j}{\alpha}P_j.
$$

Notice that all the coefficients on both sides are non-negative and sum to $1$. Hence, both sides represent convex combinations of points in $S_1$ and $S_2$, respectively.

Therefore, there exists a point that belongs to both convex hulls. Hence,
$$
\operatorname{conv}(S_1)\cap\operatorname{conv}(S_2)\neq\varnothing.
$$

Since two sets whose convex hulls intersect cannot be separated by a linear hyperplane, the partitions $S_1$ and $S_2$ cannot be separated by a linear classifier.

## VC Theory

VC Theory is one of the foundational theories in learning.

Let us introduce the Universe and Ranges.

We denote Universe with '$\mathbb{X}$' and Ranges with '$\mathcal{R}$'.
$$
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{R})
$$
where, $\mathcal{R} \subseteq 2^{\mathbb{X}}$. $\mathcal{R}$ is the collection of subsets of the universe. This tuple together is called a **set system**.

### Projection

Given that $\mathbb{Y} \subseteq \mathbb{X}$, projection $(\mathbb{Y}, \mathcal{R_{\mathbb{Y}}})$ is defined as the following:
$$
\mathcal{R_{\mathbb{Y}}} = \{\mathcal{S} \cap \mathbb{Y} | \mathcal{S}\in \mathcal{R}\}
$$
given that $\mathcal{R_{\mathbb{Y}}} \subseteq 2^{\mathbb{Y}}$.
